Relationships shape learning

Across BSE, BEL and IBT, we have seen that students engage more when facilitators know something about their pressures, aspirations, and constraints. Relationship-building is not extra work; it is part of equitable teaching design.

Incident 01 • BSE project reality

From “unreliable teammate” to “student under pressure”

In a software project, a teammate repeatedly missed stand-ups and GitHub deadlines. The group initially read this as lack of commitment. A quick one-on-one check-in changed the story: the student was sharing a laptop with siblings and balancing demanding responsibilities outside class.

That moment reframed the group response. Instead of blame, the team redistributed tasks, added pair-programming support, and adjusted submission rhythms. This is what human-centred facilitation looks like in practice: moving from judgement to understanding, then from understanding to design.

When students feel known, support becomes more precise, fairness becomes more realistic, and participation becomes more possible.

Relational pedagogy in everyday practice

Inclusion needs structure

Psychological safety does not happen by accident. It grows when facilitators establish norms, model respectful tone, and create multiple entry points for students with different confidence levels, cultures, and prior learning experiences.

Co-created norms

  • Be present — reduce distractions and show respect.
  • Step up, step down — make room for different voices.
  • Critique ideas, not people — keep challenge rigorous and humane.

Inclusive participation

Students arrive with different educational histories. Some are comfortable debating; others are speaking up for the first time. Low-stakes prompts, pair shares, anonymous polls, and reflective writing help widen participation without forcing performance.

Tone as pedagogy

The facilitator’s phrasing, non-verbals, and response to error all communicate what kind of room this is. A respectful tone can lower the risk of participation; sarcasm or public shaming can close the room immediately.

When facilitators should step in

Open dialogue matters, especially in diverse institutions. But freedom to speak does not mean freedom to demean. Facilitators need clear thresholds for intervention, especially when controversial issues intersect with identity, power, and harm.

Incident 02 • entrepreneurship studio

Challenging the idea without diminishing the person

During a venture critique, one student responded to a peer’s model with, “That’s stupid; no investor would fund that.” The room went quiet. The facilitator intervened immediately by naming the behaviour, returning to the agreed norm, and modelling a more constructive way to disagree: “Try: I’m not convinced yet — can you show us the revenue logic?”

This is a strong example of a timely intervention that preserves dignity while protecting academic challenge. The goal is not to avoid critique, but to keep critique ethical and usable.

Three intervention thresholds

  • Safety threshold: Step in when comments become harmful, disrespectful, or dehumanising.
  • Accuracy threshold: Correct clear misinformation, especially on evidence-based issues.
  • Values threshold: Re-anchor the discussion when viewpoints undermine dignity, justice, or institutional mission.

The third programme-BEL

Beyond BSE and IBT, the BEL perspective adds an important leadership and behavioural dimension to safe learning environments. It reminds us that inclusion is not only about course delivery, but also about how people feel seen, heard, and empowered within teams and institutions.

Programme lens 03 • BEL perspective

Leadership, belonging, and the emotional climate of learning

From a BEL point of view, students do not just respond to content; they respond to the climate around the content. A team can have a strong task plan and still struggle if some members feel ignored, spoken over, or invisible.

This lens helps us notice quieter forms of exclusion: one student constantly taking over discussion, another member being reduced to note-taking, or a peer withdrawing after repeated dismissive comments.

What does this mean?

  • Belonging: Students are more likely to engage when they feel valued within the group, not merely present in it.
  • Emotional intelligence: Facilitators and peers need awareness of tone, power, and how feedback lands emotionally.
  • Leadership practice: Safe classrooms are sustained when students themselves help uphold norms and shared responsibility.

Controversial issues are already in the room

In ALU spaces, difficult questions appear in venture design, AI ethics, labour models, climate decisions, financing systems, gender inclusion, and governance debates. Avoiding them does not remove them; it only pushes students to make sense of them without structured inquiry, evidence, or facilitation.

Safe spaces still make room for challenge, but the challenge is structured, respectful, and evidence-aware.

Supportive institutions make inclusive facilitation easier through policy, case banks, training, and student feedback loops.

What ALU should strengthen

If we want safe and inclusive learning to be more than facilitator personality, institutional systems must make it easier to teach well under pressure, across difference, and through controversy.

1

Shared guidance

Clear protocols on controversial issues, classroom norms, and escalation pathways would reduce uncertainty for faculty and students.

2

Faculty development

Practical workshops on de-escalation, inclusive facilitation, student voice, and psychologically safe feedback would strengthen consistency.

3

Contextual case banks

ALU-specific examples from tech, entrepreneurship, and social impact would help faculty teach difficult issues with relevance and nuance.

4

Student feedback loops

Mid-course check-ups on belonging, voice, and classroom climate would help facilitators improve while the learning is still happening.
